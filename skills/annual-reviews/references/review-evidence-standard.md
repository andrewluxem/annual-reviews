# Annual review evidence standard

Use this reference to decide what a review may state, how strongly it may state it, and what must remain an open slot.

## Evidence types

| Type | What it can support | Required handling |
|---|---|---|
| Dated work evidence | A contribution, behavior, result, or missed commitment | Preserve the date, scope, and source supplied |
| Attributed feedback | Another person's observation or experience | Preserve attribution unless the input says it is anonymous |
| Aggregated anonymous feedback | A theme across protected feedback | State the theme and evidence count only if supplied; never infer a source |
| Self-reflection | The subject's view of strengths, growth, or motivation | Label it as reflection rather than independent proof |
| Inference | A possible explanation or pattern | Label it and keep it out of factual or rating claims |
| Missing evidence | A claim the notes cannot support | Use a visible slot naming the exact fact needed |

An artifact is not evidence merely because it is detailed. Check whether the user supplied it as a real record, a recollection, a draft, or a hypothetical.

## Build an evidence ledger

Give every material item an ID. Record:

1. date or period;
2. source type;
3. observable contribution or behavior;
4. supported result or impact;
5. role expectation, value, or competency it informs;
6. missing context or contrary evidence.

Use the IDs in the review draft. This makes unsupported language easy to find and gives an authorized reviewer a path back to the source notes.

## Cover the review period

- Look for evidence from the beginning, middle, and end of the period.
- Separate recurring patterns from one incident.
- Distinguish work completed by the person from work completed by a team.
- Include material constraints and dependencies when the input supplies them.
- Consider evidence that complicates the strongest positive or negative claim.

Do not fill an empty part of the period with an invented example. State `Earlier-period evidence needed` or another precise gap.

## Write strengths and growth areas

A useful strength statement contains:

1. the specific behavior or capability;
2. one or more supplied examples;
3. the supported impact;
4. the context in which the strength is most useful.

A useful growth statement contains:

1. the observable behavior or missing capability;
2. the supplied situation or pattern;
3. the supported impact;
4. a measurable next action and support plan.

Avoid identity labels such as careless, difficult, natural leader, or not strategic. Replace the label with what the person did, what happened, and what evidence is still needed.

## Check common review distortions

- **Recency:** recent work crowds out the rest of the period.
- **Halo:** one strong result makes every dimension sound strong.
- **Horns:** one problem makes every dimension sound weak.
- **Attribution:** a shared result becomes one person's sole result.
- **Similarity:** the reviewer treats a familiar style as better performance.
- **Visibility:** work seen by the reviewer receives more weight than quieter work.
- **Outcome:** luck or external conditions are confused with the quality of the person's decisions.

Flag a risk when the supplied evidence suggests it. Do not accuse a reviewer of bias without evidence.

## Map evidence to a rubric

Apply only the rubric the user supplies.

1. Quote or restate the relevant criterion accurately.
2. Link evidence IDs to the criterion.
3. Identify contrary or missing evidence.
4. Distinguish performance, values, potential, and overall contribution if the rubric separates them.
5. State `Rubric mapping incomplete` when the rubric or evidence is insufficient.

The skill can prepare an evidence case and map it to criteria. It must not recommend, select, or populate a rating. That employment decision belongs to an authorized human.
